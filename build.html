Build your own.

You don't need to know how to code. You describe the page and the AI writes it. Each class site was built inside one class period.

How it works

  1. Open an AI chat. Claude, ChatGPT and Gemini all work.
  2. Copy a prompt from this page, fill in the [brackets], and send it.
  3. Save the code it gives you as a file named index.html, then open that file in your browser.
  4. Ask for changes in plain words until you like it. "Make the headline shorter." "Use green instead."

Start from scratch

Five prompts, in the order we'd use them. Stop after the second one and you already have a working page.

1Get clear on the customer

Do this first. A page for "everyone" convinces no one.

I want to start a small business: [your idea in one sentence]. It would serve [who] in [town or area].

Before we build anything, help me get clear on the customer. Ask me up to five questions, one at a time, about who buys this, what problem it solves for them, and why they would pick me.

Then sum up my answers as: the customer, their problem, my offer, and three reasons to trust me. Don't invent facts about me. If I don't know something, mark it as a guess to test.

2Build the page

This is the one that makes the site. Send it in the same chat as the first prompt.

Build me a one-page website for this business. Give me one complete HTML file with the CSS and JavaScript inside it, so I can save it as index.html and open it in a browser.

The business: [name]. [What it does, in one sentence].
The customer: [who it's for] in [town or area].
The offer: [what they get and what it costs, or "help me draft pricing"].

Include these sections, in this order: a headline with one clear button, how it works in three steps, three simple plans with prices, four reasons to pick us, five common questions, and a short form to request a quote.

Rules:
- Design it for phones first. It should also look good on a laptop.
- Put every word a customer reads in one clearly marked block at the top of the script, so I can change the words without touching the layout.
- Pick two or three colors that fit the business and tell me why you chose them.
- Mark every price as a draft. Don't invent reviews, awards or numbers. If you need a fact from me, ask.
- The quote form should open the visitor's email app with their answers filled in, addressed to [your email]. Don't store anything.

3Test three headlines

The same test we ran with the Snow Crew page. One page, three headlines, see which one people click.

Add a headline test to my page. Write three versions of the headline and the button text: one that leads with the benefit, one that uses humor, and one that creates urgency.

Make the page show version A, B or C when I add ?v=a, ?v=b or ?v=c to the end of the link. Keep everything else the same.

Then give me a simple way to compare which version gets more clicks if I show it to 10 to 20 people.

4Add an instant quote calculator

Like the one on Tidy Nest. Good when the price depends on a few choices.

Add an instant quote calculator to my page. The visitor picks [the things that change the price, like size, how often, and add-ons] and the price updates right away.

Show how the price was built, line by line. Use these starting prices: [your prices, or "suggest draft prices"]. Label the result as an estimate, not a final price.

5Put it online for free

The class sites live on GitHub Pages, which is free. This prompt walks you through it.

Walk me through putting my index.html file online for free with GitHub Pages. I'm a beginner and I have never used GitHub.

Give me one step at a time and wait for me to say "done" before the next one. Start with creating an account, then a new public repository, then uploading my file, then turning on Pages in Settings. At the end, tell me what my link will be.

Start from a class page

Faster than starting from scratch. Pick a page and choose how you want to use it.

  • Remix it copies a prompt with that page's code attached. Fill in the brackets and the AI changes a working page to fit your idea.
  • Rebuild it copies a prompt that describes the page, so you can watch the AI build it from nothing.

Aggie's Pooper Scoopers

Yard cleanup for dog owners. Promo bar, three plans, quote form. Open the site

Read the rebuild prompt 
Build a one-page website for a student-run yard cleanup business. Give me one complete HTML file with the CSS and JavaScript inside it, so I can save it as index.html and open it in a browser.

Business: Aggie's Pooper Scoopers. Weekly dog waste removal for homeowners in Logan and Cache Valley, Utah. It is a class project, not an operating business.

Look: navy (#0f2a4a) and gold (#f2b632) on a cream background (#fbf7ef). System font. Rounded pill buttons. Cards with soft borders. Design it for phones first.

Sections, in this order:
1. A header that stays at the top. First a red promo bar: "Holiday Yard Reset: $20 off your first cleanup. Book by Dec 15 and have a clean yard for guests." with a "Claim it" link to the quote form. Under it, the business name on the left ("Pooper Scoopers" in gold) and a "Get a free quote" button on the right.
2. A hero on a navy background. Headline: "Get your yard back. We'll handle the poop." Under it: "Weekly dog waste removal for Cache Valley homeowners. Walk barefoot, host the BBQ, let the kids play. All without lifting a scooper." Two buttons: "Get a free quote" and "See plans". Four small badges: Locally owned in Logan. Gate always re-latched. Pet-safe, no chemicals. No contracts.
3. "How it works", three numbered cards: Tell us about your yard. We scoop on a set day. Enjoy a clean yard.
4. "A clean yard makes the holidays easier", with room for two photos side by side, each with a short caption. Skip this section if I don't give you photos.
5. "Simple plans", three cards: Every other week, $20 per visit. Weekly, $17 per visit, marked "Most popular". One-time cleanup, $75 and up. Note under them: "Draft pricing for class project. Extra dogs add a small per-visit fee. Final quote after we see your yard."
6. "Why homeowners pick Aggie's", four cards: Locals who care. Safe for your pets. Reliable and easy. Fair and simple.
7. "Common questions", five that open when tapped: What areas do you serve? Do I need to be home? What about snow? What if my dog is nervous around strangers? Where does the waste go?
8. "Get your free quote", a navy section with a form: name, phone or email, street address or neighborhood, how many dogs, yard size, anything we should know. Sending it opens the visitor's email app with the answers filled in, addressed to [your email]. Nothing is stored.
9. Footer: "© 2026 Aggie's Pooper Scoopers · Logan, Utah · Student business project".

Rules: put every word a customer reads in one CONFIG block at the top of the script so it is easy to edit. Write one short supporting line for each card and each answer. Don't invent reviews or numbers. Leave a spot for a hero photo, but make the page look finished without one.

Clean Can Cache Valley

Curbside trash can cleaning. Three plans plus a built-in headline test. Open the site

Read the rebuild prompt 
Build a one-page website for a trash can cleaning business. Give me one complete HTML file with the CSS and JavaScript inside it, so I can save it as index.html and open it in a browser.

Business: Clean Can Cache Valley. We scrub and sanitize trash cans at the curb for homeowners in Logan and Cache Valley, Utah. It is a class proof of concept, not an operating business.

Look: dark teal (#0a4f56) and lime (#b7e24a) on a sand background (#f6f4ee). System font. Rounded pill buttons. Cards with soft borders. Design it for phones first.

Sections, in this order:
1. A header that stays at the top. First a lime promo bar: "Launch offer: first can cleaned free with any plan." with a "Claim it" link to the quote form. Under it, the business name on the left ("Cache Valley" in lime) and a "Get a free quote" button on the right.
2. A hero on a teal background. Headline: "Your trash cans are the one chore you keep forgetting." Under it: "We scrub and sanitize your bins right at your curb, after pickup day. No smell, no flies, no hose in your driveway. Built for busy Cache Valley families." Two buttons: "Get a free quote" and "See plans". Four small badges: Local to Cache Valley. Done at your curb. No contracts. Text or email to book.
3. "How it works", three numbered cards: Tell us your pickup day. We clean after the truck. Bins back, smelling fresh.
4. Room for one wide photo with the caption "Every week they sit at the curb. We make sure they don't smell like it." Skip this section if I don't give you a photo.
5. "Why homeowners pick us", four cards: Get your weekend back. Fewer flies, fewer smells. Works around your life. Local and accountable.
6. "Simple plans", three cards: One-time clean, $35 per bin. Seasonal, $25 per bin per visit, every 4 weeks from spring to fall, marked "Most popular". Pair + save, $40 for 2 bins per visit. Note under them: "Draft pricing for a class proof of concept. Final pricing depends on bin count and route density."
7. "Common questions", five that open when tapped: Do I need to be home? Do you work in winter? What about the wash water? What areas do you serve? How do I pay?
8. "Get your free quote", a teal section with a form: name, phone or email, street address or neighborhood, how many bins, which pickup day, which plan, anything we should know. Sending it opens the visitor's email app with the answers filled in, addressed to [your email]. Nothing is stored.
9. Footer: "Clean Can Cache Valley · Logan, Utah · Class proof of concept for USU. Not yet an operating business."

Headline test: when ?v=a, ?v=b or ?v=c is added to the link, swap the headline and the main button.
A: "Your trash cans are the one chore you keep forgetting." Button: "Get a free quote".
B: "Clean bins. Zero hose. Done on your trash day." Button: "Book my first clean".
C: "Cache Valley's cleanest curb starts with your bins." Button: "See pricing and book".

Rules: put every word a customer reads in one CONFIG block at the top of the script so it is easy to edit. Write one short supporting line for each card and each answer. Don't invent reviews or numbers, and don't claim to be licensed or insured. Leave a spot for a hero photo, but make the page look finished without one.

Before you share it

  • Read everything the AI wrote. Your name goes on it.
  • Keep prices marked as drafts until they're real. Don't publish reviews, awards or numbers you can't back up.
  • Take your phone number and address out of anything you paste into an AI chat.
  • Make sure the quote form sends to your email, not someone else's.
